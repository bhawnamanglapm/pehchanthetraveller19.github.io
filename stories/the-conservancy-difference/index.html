The Conservancy Difference

Two safari camps, forty minutes apart, and why the boundary line between them changes the entire experience.

Pehchan Editorial2025-04-157 min read
  1. Home
  2. Stories
  3. The Conservancy Difference

On the reserve side, we counted fourteen vehicles at a leopard. On the conservancy side, forty minutes north, the rule is five, and on the morning we found a cheetah with cubs there were two — ours and one other, which left after fifteen minutes.

What the boundary actually is

The Maasai Mara National Reserve is government land with open access for any licensed vehicle. The conservancies bordering it are private leases: Maasai landowning families lease their land to a small number of camps, which pay per bed per night, and in exchange the land carries a strict cap on vehicles and beds.

That single structural difference produces almost everything visitors notice. Vehicle limits at sightings. Permission to drive off-road, at night, and to walk. Fewer beds across a larger area.

On the reserve side we counted fourteen vehicles at a leopard. On the conservancy side, the rule is five.

The honest trade-off

Conservancy camps cost more, sometimes considerably. And the famous Mara river crossings are in the reserve, not the conservancies — so migration-season visitors usually need to drive in for those, which means joining the crowd for a morning.

There is also a fair argument that the reserve's open access is more democratic, and that a model where exclusivity is the product deserves scrutiny rather than automatic praise. The counter-argument is the payment structure: conservancy leases put money directly into the hands of the families who own the land, monthly, whether or not the beds are full.

What I would book

Three nights in a conservancy and one long day into the reserve during migration season. Outside migration season, all conservancy. And I would ask any camp, directly, what proportion of the nightly rate reaches the landowners — the good ones answer immediately.
