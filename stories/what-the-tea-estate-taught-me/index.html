What the Tea Estate Taught Me About Slow Mornings

Three days on a working plantation above Munnar, and the argument for staying still.

Pehchan Editorial2025-09-185 min read
  1. Home
  2. Stories
  3. What the Tea Estate Taught Me About Slow Mornings

The pluckers were already out when I got to the verandah at half past six, moving across a hillside in a line that from a distance looked like weather crossing the slope. I had come to Munnar with a list of six things to do and by the second morning I had done none of them.

The mist has a schedule

It comes up the valley from the south between six and seven, fills everything below fifteen hundred metres, and burns off by nine. If you leave for Eravikulam at seven you drive through it and see nothing. If you sit on a verandah with a cup of what was growing outside forty-eight hours earlier, you watch a valley disappear and come back.

This is not a profound observation. It is a scheduling one, and scheduling is most of what separates a good trip from a rushed one.

I had come with a list of six things to do and by the second morning I had done none of them.

A working estate is not a resort

The bungalow I stayed in has six rooms and about two thousand acres of tea around it, and the tea is the business — the rooms are the sideline. That changes what the staff know. The man who took me walking at dawn had been on that estate for nineteen years and could tell me which sections were replanted in the nineties by the colour of the leaf.

You cannot buy that in a hotel with a spa. It is worth choosing for, and it is worth paying for, and it is one of the few things in travel where the premium buys something real.

The case for three nights in one place

Two nights is one full day. Three nights is two full days plus a morning you do not have to plan, and that unplanned morning is reliably the one you remember. On a two-week trip that means five stops instead of eight. You will see less. You will have been somewhere.
