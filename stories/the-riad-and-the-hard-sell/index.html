The Riad and the Hard Sell

On navigating Marrakech's commercial pressure without deciding the city is against you.

Pehchan Editorial2025-06-117 min read
  1. Home
  2. Stories
  3. The Riad and the Hard Sell

Within twenty minutes of entering the medina I had been told the square was closed, that the tanneries were open only today, and that the road I was walking down led nowhere. All three were false, delivered pleasantly, and are a standard opening.

It is a transaction, not an ambush

The framing most visitors arrive with — that this is hostility — makes the trip worse and is not accurate. Marrakech has a commission economy. Someone who redirects you to a shop earns a percentage. That is a job, and it is legal, and it is aimed at a very large number of visitors who arrive for two nights and will never come back.

Understanding it as an economic system rather than a personal one changes your response from irritation to a polite, repeatable no. Which is the only response that works.

The three things that actually helped

First: book a riad that meets you at the taxi drop. The last four minutes on foot with luggage is where most bad first impressions are manufactured, and any decent riad will send someone.

Second: do a craft walk on day one with a guide from a medina family, explicitly with no commission stops. You learn the geography of the trades — dyers here, metal there — and after that the souks stop being a maze and start being a map.

Third: buy from the workshop, not the shopfront. The price is often similar and the difference in what reaches the maker is large.

What the city gives back

On the fourth evening, on a rooftop in the northern medina with the Atlas visible and snow on it, the muezzins started across the city at slightly different moments, the way they do. Nobody was selling me anything. That is the version of Marrakech that people come back for, and you have to get through the first afternoon to reach it.
