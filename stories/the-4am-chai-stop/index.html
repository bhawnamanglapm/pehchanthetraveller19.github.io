The 4am Chai Stop

On a Himalayan road where the only thing open is a tin shed, and why that became the best part of the trip.

Pehchan Editorial2025-11-146 min read
  1. Home
  2. Stories
  3. The 4am Chai Stop

The driver stopped without asking. It was four in the morning, we were somewhere above Nako with the headlights making a small yellow room in front of us, and there was a tin shed with a bulb outside it. He turned off the engine, which is when I understood how loud the engine had been.

Inside, a man in three jackets was already boiling milk. Not for us — for himself, and for whoever came. This is the part of Himalayan road travel nobody photographs: the economy of small kindnesses that keeps a road open at four in the morning in a place where nothing else is.

What the guidebooks do not tell you about the driving days

Every itinerary for Spiti treats the driving as the cost of the destination. Eight hours to Sangla. Seven to Nako. Six to Kaza. The days are listed the way you list a customs queue — something to be endured on the way to the thing you came for.

This is wrong, and I think it is wrong in a way that matters beyond Spiti. The driving days are where the landscape actually changes. You do not arrive in the cold desert; you watch the pine give out somewhere past Reckong Peo and get replaced by nothing, and then by ochre, and then by a scale that stops making sense. If you sleep through it, you have paid for a landscape you did not see.

You do not arrive in the cold desert. You watch the pine give out and get replaced by nothing, and then by ochre.

The chai was not good

I want to be honest about this, because travel writing has a habit of improving things. It was oversweet, boiled long past the point of tea, and served in a glass that had been rinsed rather than washed. It was also the best thing I drank in eleven days, for reasons that have nothing to do with the tea.

We stood outside because the shed was too small for four people. The sky at 3,600 metres, with no moon and no town for forty kilometres, is not dark. It is crowded. Somebody said so, and then nobody said anything for about ten minutes, which on a trip with three other people is its own kind of achievement.

What I would do differently

I would build the stops in. Not the viewpoints — the viewpoints take care of themselves and are usually full of people doing the same calculation you are. I mean the unscheduled hour: leave two hours of slack in every driving day and spend it wherever the driver slows down without being asked. He knows. He has been doing this road for eleven years and he knows exactly where the good tin sheds are.

That is most of what I have learned about travelling in the mountains, and it took an oversweet glass of chai at four in the morning to work it out.
